The Quiet Harbour

By Ines Calder

The harbour at Kellan's Point has been quiet for most of the last century. The fishing fleet that once filled it left in the nineteen-sixties, and the warehouses along the north quay were turned, one by one, into flats, studios and a small museum that opens on Saturdays.

Yet every spring the water comes alive again. For three weeks in April, thousands of eels arrive from the open sea and push up the narrow channel that links the harbour to the river beyond. Nobody planned it, and for a long time nobody noticed.

It was a retired schoolteacher, Margaret Oyelaran, who first counted them. She had taken up evening walks after her husband died, and she began to keep a notebook of what she saw from the footbridge: the herons, the tide, and the strange silver ribbons moving just under the surface.

Her notebooks, now kept in the museum, record more than forty springs. They show the eels arriving later each decade, and in smaller numbers after the sluice gate was rebuilt in 1988. They also show, in her careful handwriting, the year the numbers started to climb again.

That was the year the town council agreed to open the gate at night during April. The change cost almost nothing. A volunteer turns a wheel at dusk and closes it again before the morning tide, and the eels, which travel in the dark, find the way open.

Scientists from the regional university now visit each spring with tagging equipment and underwater cameras. Their counts agree with Margaret's: the run has roughly tripled since the night openings began, and the eels are arriving earlier again.

"It is one of the clearest examples we have of a small, local decision making a measurable difference," says Dr Tomas Reyna, who leads the survey. "People tend to think conservation needs enormous budgets. Sometimes it needs a notebook and a wheel."

The museum has turned the story into an exhibition. Visitors can read the notebooks, watch footage of the night openings and, in April, join a volunteer on the footbridge after dark to watch the water for themselves.

Margaret died in 2019, a few weeks before the spring count. The volunteers who turn the wheel still keep a notebook on the footbridge, in a waterproof box screwed to the railing, and anyone who sees the eels is invited to write down what they saw.

The entries are short and mostly practical: the time, the tide, the weather, a rough count. But now and then someone adds a line of their own. The most recent one, in a child's handwriting, reads simply: lots tonight, very fast, all going the same way.
